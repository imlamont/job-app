# Reading the role type

Different fields treat different things as proof. A resume that offers the wrong *kind* of evidence reads as weak even when the underlying work is strong, because the reader is scanning for something that isn't there.

Classify the role before tailoring, say the classification out loud so the person can correct it, and let it drive what gets foregrounded.

## How to classify

Ask what this reader treats as proof, and who the reader is. A hiring manager for a performance team wants a measurement. A sales director wants attainment against quota. A design lead wants to see the work. None of them are impressed by the others' evidence.

Signals: the posting's own vocabulary, what its first responsibilities describe, what the team ships, and who is likely doing the screening — recruiter, hiring manager, or practitioner.

State it plainly before tailoring: "I'm reading this as a systems role, so I'm foregrounding measured results and profiling methodology over breadth." One line, easily corrected, and correcting it early saves a whole revision.

## Archetypes

### Systems, kernel, performance, embedded, infrastructure

**Proof is measurement.** Before-and-after numbers, units, the hardware it ran on, the baseline it was compared against. An optimization claim without a number reads as an assertion.

Foreground: benchmarks and methodology, profiling tools used, what the bottleneck actually was, how it was found. Low-level detail is a feature — naming the counter, the instruction, the cache level signals depth.

**Negative and null results are strong here.** A result that didn't work, understood precisely, often beats a success, because it demonstrates the reasoning the job requires and successes are sometimes luck.

Avoid: breadth-for-its-own-sake, long tool lists, claims that can't survive a follow-up question.

### Product and web engineering

**Proof is shipped scope.** What was built, who used it, what it replaced, whether it's in production.

Foreground: end-to-end ownership, the stack, launches, the surface area they were responsible for. Some numbers help — users, request volume, load time, conversion — but a product engineer without benchmark tables is normal, and forcing metrics onto ordinary feature work reads as padding.

Breadth is a genuine asset here, unlike in systems roles: touching frontend, backend, and deploy is evidence of usefulness on a small team.

**Negative results are neutral to slightly negative.** A story about a feature that didn't work belongs in an interview, not on the page. Reframe as what shipped and what was learned.

Avoid: framework lists with no product attached; deep internals nobody asked about.

### Data science and machine learning

**Proof is rigor plus outcome.** Model metrics against a baseline, dataset scale, whether it reached production or stayed in a notebook.

Foreground: the evaluation, not just the model. What was compared, how it was validated, what the baseline was. Production deployment is a strong differentiator because most candidates stop at the notebook.

Negative results are acceptable when framed as an experimental finding, since the field expects failed experiments.

### Research and academic

**Proof is publication and independence.** Papers, venues, citations, what was owned versus contributed, funding, advisors.

Foreground: the research question and the contribution, in that order. Methodology detail matters. Publication venue carries weight that no self-description matches.

Null results are entirely legitimate here.

### Design and UX

**Proof is the portfolio, and the resume's job is to get it opened.** Link prominently.

Foreground: the problem, the process, the outcome. What changed for users, ideally measured — task completion, retention, support volume. Craft claims without artifacts to look at are unverifiable.

Avoid: tool lists as the main content. Everyone has Figma.

### Product management

**Proof is outcomes and scope.** Metrics moved, launches owned, the size and complexity of what was coordinated.

Foreground: the decision and its result — what was chosen, what was cut, what happened. Cross-functional scope (engineers, designers, regions, stakeholders) establishes level.

Avoid: describing team activity rather than the person's own decisions.

### Sales, account management, business development

**Proof is attainment, and the format is nearly standardized.** Read in seconds by someone who knows exactly what to look for.

Foreground, in roughly this order: quota attainment as a percentage, the quota size, ranking among peers ("#2 of 34 reps"), deal or account size, sales cycle length, notable logos closed, new versus expansion mix, ramp time, President's Club or equivalent.

Attainment percentage without the quota size is incomplete — 120% of $400K and 120% of $4M are different jobs. Territory and segment matter because they establish the level of the role.

**Do not put negative results on a sales resume.** A lost deal analyzed thoughtfully is a strength in an interview and a liability on the page. The genre does not read charitably.

Avoid: process and methodology descriptions where numbers belong; responsibility language ("managed a territory") instead of results.

### Operations, finance, analysis

**Proof is quantified improvement.** Cost reduced, time saved, error rate cut, throughput raised, scale handled.

Foreground: the process before and after, the recurring value of the change, the scope of what was touched. Accuracy and compliance claims carry weight where the stakes are real.

Avoid: describing routine duties. Every analyst built dashboards; what changed because of this one?

## Hybrid and unfamiliar roles

Many postings straddle two archetypes — an ML infrastructure role wants measurement and production deployment; a sales engineer wants technical depth and revenue impact. Pick the dominant one from the first responsibilities, borrow from the second, and say what the blend is.

For a role matching none of these, derive it rather than forcing a fit. Ask: what would make this reader believe someone is good at this job? What would a skeptical practitioner in this field want to see? What do the strong people in this field put on their resumes? Then apply that, and say what assumption is being made so the person can correct it.

**When the person knows the field better than you do, defer.** Someone applying in their own industry has seen what gets responses. Ask them what the reader looks for first.
## Cross-cutting

**Numbers are not universally good.** They are the currency in sales, systems, growth, and operations; supporting evidence in product and data; and actively suspicious in design and research when they look manufactured. In the fields where numbers aren't the currency, a metric that reads as invented costs more than no metric at all.

**Bullet grammar shifts by field.** Sales bullets lead with the number, because the number is the achievement and a verb in front of it wastes the line. Systems bullets lead with the action and land the number, because the mechanism is half the claim. Design bullets lead with the problem. Match the genre.

**Depth versus breadth is a field-level convention, not a universal virtue.** Systems and research reward depth; product engineering, early-stage roles, and PM reward range; sales rewards consistency over time. Don't apply one field's standard to another field's resume.
