# Interview prep from the posting

Goal: a short, prioritized list of what's actually worth reviewing. Not a curriculum. The person has limited time and usually a date on the calendar.

## Where the topics come from

**The posting's first responsibilities.** What the team does daily is what they'll ask about. A posting centered on distributed systems produces different questions than one centered on data modeling, regardless of the shared "strong CS fundamentals" boilerplate.

**Their own resume.** Every technology and project on the tailored resume is fair game, and this is the most commonly underprepared area. Someone who listed a project two years ago and hasn't thought about it since will stumble on "walk me through the hardest bug you hit there." Flag the resume items most likely to be probed.

**The gap between the two.** Where the posting requires something they're weak in, it's worth knowing enough to discuss it honestly and show they could pick it up. Not enough to fake depth — which fails immediately with a competent interviewer.

**Anything downgraded or dropped from the resume.** A claim weakened for lack of evidence, or a keyword removed as too risky, is still a live topic — the underlying work happened and may well come up. The person should know what they can defend and where the line is. Numbers still sitting as placeholders are the same: they'll be asked.

**The company's known process,** if they already know it or want it looked up. Some companies have well-documented loops. Offer to search; don't assume.

## Output shape

A prioritized list, roughly:

**Likely to come up** — three to five topics, each with a line on why it's likely and what specifically to review.

**Your resume, under questioning** — which two or three items they should be ready to go deep on, with the questions most likely to be asked about each.

**Worth a skim** — things mentioned in the posting that might appear.

**Questions to ask them** — two or three real ones derived from the posting, the kind that come from having read it closely. Not "what's the culture like."

Keep the whole thing scannable. If they want depth on any one topic, they'll ask, and that's a better conversation than a document that tries to cover everything.

## What not to do

Don't produce a generic list of data structures and algorithms. If the loop includes a coding round, say so and point at the topics the posting's domain suggests, but a full DS&A syllabus isn't what this skill is for.

Don't script answers for behavioral questions. Help them identify which of their real experiences fit the common question shapes — a conflict, a failure, something they owned — and let them tell it in their own words. Memorized answers sound memorized.
