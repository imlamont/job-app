# Cover letters and outreach

Three different forms with different readers. Don't blur them.

## Cover letters

Most are read for thirty seconds, if at all. Some are read closely — small companies, research groups, and roles where writing matters. Write as though it will be read, keep it short enough that thirty seconds is enough.

**Structure that works:**

1. What role, and the one-sentence reason this person and this team fit
2. The single most relevant thing they've done, told as a short specific story — what the problem was, what they did, what happened
3. Something concrete about this company or team that isn't in the posting, and why it matters to them
4. A short close

Three to four short paragraphs. No page-filling.

**What kills a cover letter:**

- Restating the resume in prose — the reader already has the resume
- Praise that applies to any company ("your commitment to innovation")
- Enthusiasm words doing the work that specifics should do ("incredibly excited," "deeply passionate")
- Explaining why the job is good for *them* rather than why they're good for the job
- Apologizing for gaps, or preemptively defending a weakness nobody asked about

**Paragraph 3 requires real information.** If the person doesn't know anything specific about the company, offer to research it — this is the natural moment to ask. If they decline and have nothing specific to say, cut the paragraph rather than filling it with generic flattery. A three-paragraph letter beats a four-paragraph letter with a hollow one in the middle.

**Voice.** Write it in the person's register, not a formal template voice. Ask for a sample of their writing if any is available. A letter that doesn't sound like them falls apart in the first phone call.

**Claims that imply you've done something must be true before sending.** A letter that references a company's architecture implies the person has read their docs. That's a strong move and it's also a trap: it will be tested in the first screen. Flag any such sentence in the draft and tell them explicitly to go read the thing before sending — thirty minutes, and then the claim is true and they can hold the conversation.

## Referral requests

Asking a human for a favor. Different rules entirely.

**Short.** Five sentences is generous. Long asks read as high-effort to grant.

**Make it easy to say yes.** Name the exact role and link it. Attach the resume. Say explicitly that a "no" or a "I don't know you well enough" is completely fine — this makes it much easier for them to help, because the social risk drops.

**Be honest about the relationship.** Someone who met them once at a career fair should say that. Pretending to a closer connection than exists is transparent and costs more than it gains.

**Give them the material to work with.** One or two sentences on why this role in particular — enough that they can paste it into the internal referral form, which usually asks why they're recommending this person.

**Don't ask for a referral in the first message to a stranger.** Ask about the team, or the work, and let the conversation get there.

## Recruiter and cold outreach

**Lead with the specific thing.** What they've built, in one line, connected to what that company is working on. Not a summary of their whole background.

**One clear ask.** A short call, a pointer to the right person, a look at their application. Not "any advice you have."

**Match the channel's length.** LinkedIn messages and cold emails are short. A connection request note is one or two sentences.

**Follow up once**, after a week or so, and then stop. A single short follow-up is normal. More than that reads badly and is remembered.

## Writing to a specific person

Different from applying through a portal, and worth more. When the person names a contact, research who that contact actually is before drafting — title, org, location, what they've posted publicly. This changes the message substantially, and someone described vaguely ("might be able to help with hiring") often turns out to run the org the req reports into.

**Their own public postings are the best available signal.** A hiring manager who has advertised for specific expertise has told you what they're short on. Lead with whatever of the person's work matches that.

**Ask for a conversation, not a referral.** Someone senior who doesn't know the person can say yes to fifteen minutes and has no basis for vouching for them. The ask has to be something they can actually grant.

**Respect the channel's constraints.** LinkedIn connection requests cap at 300 characters, so the short variant has to survive standalone rather than being a trimmed version of the long one.

**Offer variants at different risk levels**, and be honest about what each requires:

- *Short* — fits a connection request. Credentials and the ask, nothing else.
- *Lead with the result* — the strongest technical story, told concretely, then the ask.
- *Ask about their problem* — a real question about the work, informed by having read their docs. Most likely to get a reply from someone senior, because it asks about them instead of about you. Only send it if the reading actually happened.

**Timing beats polish.** When there's runway before the req closes, having something concrete to point at — a merged PR against their repo, a benchmark on their platform — changes the message from a request for guidance into a conversation between engineers. Say when it's worth waiting for that, and when the req looks likely to close first.
